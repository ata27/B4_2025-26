## Chapter 1: Basic Physical and Chemical Structure

**1.** Calculate the mass of the atmosphere. Mean radius of earth = 6371 km and acceleration due to gravity (g) is 9.8 m s⁻².

<details>
<summary><b>Show answer</b></summary>


The mass of the atmosphere can be most easily calculated by knowing that average surface pressure is 1 atmosphere (101325 Pa) and that the atmospheric pressure at the surface of Earth is equal to the force of the atmosphere spread over the surface. Force of the atmosphere on the surface is just the mass of the atmosphere multiplied by the acceleration due to gravity. From this one needs to work out the area of the Earth. Assuming a sphere we have:

p = F/A = (mass × g) / Area

mass = p·A/g = 101325 (Pa or kg m⁻¹ s²) × 4π(6371×10³)² (m²) / 9.8 (m s⁻²)

**mass = 5.25 × 10¹⁸ kg**


</details>

**2.** What is the difference between the mass mixing ratio of CO₂ in air and the volume mixing ratio of CO₂ in air?

<details>
<summary><b>Show answer</b></summary>


*The mass mixing ratio involves the molar mass of CO₂ in comparison to the mass of air. The molar mass of air can be approximated as 0.2×MrO₂ + 0.8×MrN₂ = 28.9 g/mol.*

*When considering the volume mixing ratio we make the assumption that ALL gases occupy the same molar volume. Hence, to convert between volume mixing ratios (sometimes called mole fractions) we then make use of the molar masses of gases to calculate their burdens in grams (or Tg or Gg).*


</details>

**3.** By what % will the mass change by doubling of the amount of CO₂ in the atmosphere from the present mixing ratio of 400 ppm?

<details>
<summary><b>Show answer</b></summary>


Need to work out the mass of CO₂ in the atmosphere.

Calculate the number of moles of atmosphere = Mass/MR of air (0.8×N₂ + 0.2×O₂) = 1.82×10²⁰ moles

Mass of CO₂ = 400×10⁻⁶ × moles of atmosphere × 44×10⁻³ (kg/mol) = 3.2×10¹⁵ kg

New mass of atmosphere = 5.25×10¹⁸ kg + 3.2×10¹⁵ kg = 5.253×10¹⁸ kg (or **0.06%**).


</details>

**6.** Explain why mixing ratios are a useful concept in atmospheric chemistry?

<details>
<summary><b>Show answer</b></summary>


Mixing ratios tell us about the fraction of a chemical species in the mixture of gases in the atmosphere. If the atmosphere was comprised of non-interacting ideal gases the mixing ratios should never change. If the mixing ratios do change they tell us that processes are affecting the gases in the atmosphere. Increases in mixing ratio tell us about sources (production). Decreases in mixing ratio tell us about sinks (loss).


</details>

**11.** We saw in Exercise 5 that we can relate bi-molecular and ter-molecular rate constants by considering the role of pressure quenching. The rate coefficients for the following reactions exhibit a pressure dependence over the range 0.001–1 bar.

- OH + HNO₃ → NO₃ + H₂O, ΔH° = −75 kJ mol⁻¹
- OH + NO₂ + M → HNO₃ + M, ΔH° = −207 kJ mol⁻¹

Explain in broad terms the origin of these pressure dependencies. Sketch the potential energy curves for the reaction pathway in each case, considering the different nature of the products formed. Why do both rate constants increase as the temperature decreases?

<details>
<summary><b>Show answer</b></summary>


Both reactions are exothermic. So based on Le Chatelier's principle we would expect the forward reactions to be disfavoured at equilibrium. However, this is an argument based on thermodynamics not kinetics! This question wants us to think about the causes for the kinetics of the reaction which are attributed to the mechanism.

In general, for a reaction to be disfavoured at higher temperatures implies that there is an intermediate that is formed along the reaction trajectory which can easily "decompose" back to the reactants as temperature increases.

A + B → [A–B]*

[A–B]* → A + B (increases at higher temperatures MORE than the increase in the rate of collisions between A and B)

[A–B]* + M → Products

In this simple mechanism M (pressure) is involved in stabilising the intermediate and forming the product. As [M] (pressure) increases then more of the intermediate is converted into product molecules.


</details>

## Chapter 3: Atmospheric Photochemistry

**12.** Give a brief account of the factors which determine the photolysis rates of different constituents in the earth's atmosphere.

<details>
<summary><b>Show answer</b></summary>


J = F × σ × φ

**F (flux of photons)** — higher closer to the Sun. Exponential decrease in F with optical depth.

F_z = F_TOA × exp(−OD)

OD in the UV is comprised of O₂ and O₃ mainly. To work out OD need to know cross section (σ) and column. Can only work out columns of well mixed gases and σ typically come from lab data.

**σ (cross section)** — discussed above. It's possible to relate the cross section to the electronic transitions in a molecule. Some cross-sections are very structured, others very broad. Typically cross sections are larger than the cross-sectional area of a molecule.

**φ (quantum yield)** — similar to a probability of when a molecule absorbs a photon of sufficient energy that it "photo"-dissociates (breaks apart). Typically assume unity (1). Quantum yields have an apparent dependence on temperature which is related to the distribution of energy states in a molecule, where at higher temperatures there are more molecules in higher energy states and so require less excitation to cross thresholds of dissociation.


</details>

## Chapter 4: Stratospheric Ozone Chemistry

**14.** The Chapman reactions can be written as:

| Reaction | Rate/photolysis coefficient |
|---|---|
| O₂ + hν → O + O | *J₂* ≈ 5 × 10⁻¹⁰ s⁻¹ |
| O + O₂ + M → O₃ + M | *k₃* = 6 × 10⁻³⁴(300/T)² cm⁶ s⁻¹ |
| O₃ + hν → O + O₂ | *J₃* ≈ 2 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ |
| O + O₃ → O₂ + O₂ | *k₄* = 1.0 × 10⁻¹¹ exp(−2100/T) cm³ s⁻¹ |

Use this reaction scheme to derive an expression for the equilibrium ozone concentration. State any assumptions that you make.

<details>
<summary><b>Show answer</b></summary>


Putting Ox into steady state (equilibrium):

d[Ox]/dt = 2J₂[O₂] − 2k₄[O][O₃] = 0 at steady state.

2J₂[O₂] / 2k₄[O] = [O₃]. Now need an expression for [O].

From rapid interconversion of Ox we have:

J₃[O₃] = k₃[O][M][O₂]

J₃[O₃] / (k₃[M][O₂]) = [O]

**[O₃] = √( J₂k₃[O₂][O₂][M] / (k₄J₃) )**


</details>

**15.** How does this expression account for the layer-like structure of ozone in the atmosphere?

<details>
<summary><b>Show answer</b></summary>


Ozone shows a strong peak in the stratosphere at ~30 km. At altitudes lower than this, J₂ becomes very small as the optical depth increases exponentially. So [O₃] should decrease as altitude decreases. However, as altitude decreases [M] increases. And so there is a trade-off where the numerator has two competing factors, one which increases with altitude (J₂) and one which decreases with altitude ([O₂][O₂][M]).


</details>

<details>
<summary><i>Supervisor notes</i></summary>

*Supervisors: You may want to discuss which is more strongly exponential of the two terms in the numerator. Also, you may want to get the students to think about what would happen to the [O₃] if [O₂] was increased. Would the altitude of peak ozone increase or decrease?*

</details>

**18.** Nitrogen oxides (NOx = NO + NO₂) give rise to odd oxygen loss in the stratosphere and production in the free troposphere. Odd oxygen loss depends on the following reactions:

| Reaction | Rate/photolysis coefficient |
|---|---|
| O₃ + hν → O₂ + O | J₁ = 10⁻³ s⁻¹ |
| O + O₂ + M → O₃ + M | k₂ = 10⁻³³ cm⁶ s⁻¹ |
| NO + O₃ → NO₂ + O₂ | k₃ = 4 × 10⁻¹⁵ cm³ s⁻¹ |
| NO₂ + O → NO + O₂ | k₄ = 10⁻¹¹ cm³ s⁻¹ |
| NO₂ + hν → NO + O | J₅ = 10⁻² s⁻¹ |

Derive an expression for the odd oxygen loss rate, identifying the rate limiting step and the 'null' cycle. Explain how the loss cycle depends on altitude.

<details>
<summary><b>Show answer</b></summary>


d[Ox]/dt = +J₅[NO₂] − k₃[NO][O₃] − k₄[O][NO₂] = 0 at steady state.

Now we can re-write this by putting NO₂ into steady state:

d[NO₂]/dt = −J₅[NO₂] − k₄[O][NO₂] + k₃[NO][O₃] = 0 at steady state.

So, J₅[NO₂] = k₃[NO][O₃] − k₄[O][NO₂]

Now putting this into d[Ox]/dt gives us:

**d[Ox]/dt = −2k₄[O][NO₂]**

Hence step 4 is rate limiting. This also makes sense as [O] is small.

One would expect that this odd Ox loss cycle will change with altitude as [O₃] and [O] are altitude dependent. Given the role of [Ox] in this we would expect the cycle to be less efficient at lower and higher altitudes.

Null cycle:

NO + O₃ → NO₂ + O₂

NO₂ + hν → NO + O

O + O₂ + M → O₃ + M


</details>

<details>
<summary><i>Supervisor notes</i></summary>

*Supervisors: You may also want to get the students to think about the sources of NOx. The main source is from N₂O + O(¹D). O(¹D) peaks where O₃ peaks so this means that the production of NOx peaks right where O₃ is and so O₃ loss via NOx peaks there too.*

</details>

**19.** Satellite measurements of HNO₃ and NO₂ are available at 40 km, where [NO₂] = 1 × 10⁹ cm⁻³, and [HNO₃] = 4 × 10⁸ cm⁻³. Derive the concentration of OH at 40 km given the following important reactions.

|---|---|

| HNO₃ + hν → OH + NO₂ | J₂ |

Data: In the spectral region where there are incident solar photons at 40 km, the mean absorption cross section of HNO₃ is 1 × 10⁻²¹ cm², the quantum yield for OH formation is unity, and the photon intensity averaged over the same wavelength region is 3.5 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻² s⁻¹.

Justify the use of any steady state relationship in your derivation by an analysis of the time scale for steady state.

### § 4.4 HOx

**20.** Discuss the role of OH in controlling the distribution of stratospheric ozone. Your answer should include both direct and indirect effects.

<details>
<summary><b>Show answer</b></summary>


**Direct effects:**

OH reacts with O₃ and so directly lowers the [O₃] in the stratosphere.

**Indirect effects:**

OH reacts with HCl and so increases the amount of H₂O and Cl:

OH + HCl → H₂O + Cl

H₂O + O(¹D) → OH + OH

Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂

Through this mechanism one could imagine an increase in loss of O₃.

OH can also react with HONO₂ (HNO₃):

OH + HNO₃ → H₂O + NO₃

NO₃ + hν → NO₂ + O

NO₂ + hν → NO + O

O + O₂ + M → O₃ + M

So one can imagine a situation where this could lead to an increase in O₃.


</details>

**21.** In the upper stratosphere hydrogen radicals destroy odd-oxygen by the following reactions:

| Reaction | Rate/photolysis coefficient |
|---|---|
| H + O₂ + M → HO₂ + M | k₃ = 8.0 × 10⁻³² cm⁶ s⁻¹ |
| HO₂ + O → OH + O₂ | k₄ = 6.0 × 10⁻¹¹ cm³ s⁻¹ |
| OH + O₃ → HO₂ + O₂ | k₅ = 3.0 × 10⁻¹¹ cm³ s⁻¹ |
| OH + O → H + O₂ | k₆ = 3.0 × 10⁻¹¹ cm³ s⁻¹ |

Show that the rate of odd-oxygen destruction by these reactions is given by d[Ox]/dt = −2k₆[OH][O].

<details>
<summary><b>Show answer</b></summary>


d[Ox]/dt = −k4[HO₂][O] − k5[H][O₃] − k6[OH][O] &nbsp;&nbsp;(A)

If we look below we see that [O] = [O₃] = 2×10¹⁰ cm⁻³.

Using this we can calculate lifetimes of the HOx species.

τ_HO2 ≅ 1/k4[O] = 1.2 seconds

Thus it's safe to make the approximation that HOx is in steady state. It is in equilibrium as the reactions k3–k6 just interconvert HOx (HOx = H + OH + HO₂).

d[HO₂]/dt = k3[H][O₂][M] − k4[HO₂][O] = 0

d[OH]/dt = k4[HO₂][O] + k5[H][O₃] − k6[OH][O] = 0

d[H]/dt = k6[OH][O] − k3[H][O₂][M] − k5[H][O₃] = 0

So, k3[H][O₂][M] = k4[HO₂][O] &

k4[HO₂][O] + k5[H][O₃] = k6[OH][O] &

k6[OH][O] = k3[H][O₂][M] + k5[H][O₃] or

k5[H][O₃] = k6[OH][O] − k3[H][O₂][M]

So we can re-write (A) as:

d[Ox]/dt = −k4[HO₂][O] − k5[H][O₃] − k6[OH][O]
= −k3[H][O₂][M] − (k6[OH][O] − k3[H][O₂][M]) − k6[OH][O]

**= −2k6[OH][O]**

Calculate the rate of odd-oxygen destruction for the altitude where [O] = [O₃] = 2 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³ and [HOx] = [H] + [OH] + [HO₂] = 6 × 10⁶ cm⁻³. [M] = 8×10¹⁵ cm⁻³.

The trick here is that we need to calculate [H], [HO₂] and [OH].

From above we have:

[H] = k6[OH][O] / (k3[O₂][M] + k5[O₃])

[OH] = (k4[HO2][O] + k5[H][O₃]) / (k6[O])

[HO₂] = k3[H][O₂][M] / (k4[O])

One way to do this is to think about ratios:

[HOx]/[H] = [HO₂]/[H] + [OH]/[H] + 1

[H]/[OH] = k6[O] / {k3[O₂][M] + k5[O₃]} = 0.369

[H]/[HO₂] = k4[O] / k3[O₂][M] = 1.172

Now can calculate the ratios and we know total HOx = 6×10⁶ cm⁻³.

[HOx] = ([HO₂]/[H] + [OH]/[H] + 1)[H]

**[H] = 1.315×10⁶ cm⁻³**

**[OH] = 3.561×10⁶ cm⁻³**

**[HO₂] = 1.122×10⁶ cm⁻³**

**−d[Ox]/dt = 4.3×10⁶ cm⁻³ s⁻¹**


</details>

**22.** HOx in the stratosphere is mainly produced, removed and interconverted by the following series of reactions:

| # | Reaction | Rate/photolysis coefficient |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | O(¹D) + H₂O → OH + OH | k₁ = 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ cm³ s⁻¹ |
| 2 | HO₂ + OH → H₂O + O₂ | k₂ = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ cm³ s⁻¹ |
| 3 | OH + O₃ → HO₂ + O₂ | k₃ = 3.0 × 10⁻¹¹ cm³ s⁻¹ |
| 4 | HO₂ + O → OH + O₂ | k₄ = 6.0 × 10⁻¹¹ cm³ s⁻¹ |
| 5 | HO₂ + NO → NO₂ + OH | k₅ = 1.3 × 10⁻¹³ cm³ s⁻¹ |
| 6 | NO₂ + hν → NO + O | J₆ |
| 7 | O + O₂ + M → O₃ + M | k₇ |

Show that the rate of odd oxygen loss resulting from reactions [3]–[7] is given by 2k₄[HO₂][O] and calculate the rate of odd oxygen loss from these reactions at 40 km.

<details>
<summary><b>Show answer</b></summary>


d[Ox]/dt = −k3[O₃][OH] − k4[O][HO₂] + J6[NO₂] &nbsp;&nbsp;(A)

Assuming NO₂ is in steady state:

J6[NO₂] = k5[NO][HO₂] &nbsp;&nbsp;(B)

Assuming HOx is in steady state:

k1[O(1D)][H₂O] = k2[HO₂][OH] &nbsp;&nbsp;(C) — production and loss of HOx

k3[O₃][OH] = k4[HO₂][O] + k5[HO₂][NO] &nbsp;&nbsp;(D) — interconversion

The steady state says that [HO₂] = A×[OH] where A is just a number — a ratio.

Putting (D) and (B) into A gives us the desired result.


</details>

**25.** Calculate the rate of odd oxygen destruction from the following mechanism:

| Reaction | Rate/photolysis coefficient |
|---|---|
| Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂ | k₃ = 1.0 × 10⁻¹¹ cm³ s⁻¹ |
| ClO + O → Cl + O₂ | k₄ = 4.0 × 10⁻¹¹ cm³ s⁻¹ |
| ClO + NO → NO₂ + Cl | k₅ = 2.0 × 10⁻¹¹ cm³ s⁻¹ |
| NO₂ + hν → NO + O | J₆ |
| O + O₂ + M → O₃ + M | k₂[M] = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ cm³ s⁻¹ |
| Cl + CH₄ → HCl + CH₃ | k₇ = 4.0 × 10⁻¹³ cm³ s⁻¹ |

<details>
<summary><b>Show answer</b></summary>


Based on these data we need to calculate [Cl], [ClO], [O] and [NO₂].

ClOx is put into steady state through reactions 7 & 8.

k7[Cl][CH₄] = k8[HCl][OH] &nbsp;&nbsp;(B)

The Cl/ClO ratio is set by reactions 3, 4 and 5.

k3[Cl][O₃] = k4[ClO][O] + k5[ClO][NO] &nbsp;&nbsp;(C)

[Cl]/[ClO] = (k4[O] + k5[NO]) / (k3[O₃]) &nbsp;&nbsp;(D)
